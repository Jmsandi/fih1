Mini-lecture0:15–0:35

Entrepreneurs solve problems

Entrepreneurship is the process of identifying opportunities, solving problems, creating value, and organizing resources to provide goods or services that people need or want.

Not this

“What business can I start?”

Ask this

“What problem exists, who experiences it, and how can it be solved better?”

Read the definition once, then spend your time on the reframe. Entrepreneurship starts earlier than most people think: it begins with recognising a problem people actually experience.

An entrepreneur notices something that is difficult, expensive, slow, inconvenient, inaccessible, unreliable, frustrating or inefficient, and asks: "Can this be solved in a better way?"

Ask the room which of the two questions they have been asking themselves so far. Today is about seeing problems as potential opportunities, not about starting a company.

Make the problem specific

Too vagueSpecific enough to investigate
Transportation

“Transportation is bad.”

Workers in Waterloo who commute to Freetown struggle to find reliable transportation during peak morning hours.

Education

“Education is a problem.”

Some secondary-school students struggle with mathematics because they can't access affordable after-school tutoring.

Small business

“Small businesses need technology.”

Small retail businesses struggle to track customer debts because they rely on handwritten notebooks.

Read the vague statement aloud, then ask the room: Who? Where? When? Let them improve it before you reveal the specific version.

The transport example works well because everyone knows the Waterloo to Freetown commute. Point out what the specific version tells us: who, where, what, when.

Key line: a vague problem leads to a vague customer. A specific problem is something an entrepreneur can actually investigate.

Mini-lecture0:15–0:35

Start at the problem, not the app

Example: market vendors and their regular customers.

Problem

Market vendors lose customers because they can't easily tell regulars when new stock arrives.

Idea

A WhatsApp notification service for market vendors.

Solution

A tool that keeps customer lists and automatically sends stock updates through WhatsApp.

Business model

How the solution earns money. Later in the course.

When you hear “I want to build an app”, ask: What problem will it solve? Who has it? How do they solve it today?

Draw the chain on the board: Problem, Idea, Solution, Business model. The mistake many new entrepreneurs make is starting at the end.

Practise the three questions out loud with the class. When someone says "I want to build an app", respond with: What problem will it solve? Who has that problem? How are they solving it today?

The curriculum names jumping straight to a solution without a clear problem as a common mistake. Keep coming back to this throughout the day.

Mini-lecture0:15–0:35

Why start with a problem?

Problems create demandPeople with a genuine problem have a reason to look for a solution.
Problems define the customerA specific problem points to exactly who needs the solution.
Problems limit wasted spendingChecking first reduces the risk of building something nobody wants.
Problems reveal competitionPeople already cope somehow. Those workarounds are your alternatives.
Problems lead to valueThe goal isn't to create something. It's to create value.

Skip or shorten this slide if you are short of time.

Ask: has anyone seen a person or business spend money on something nobody wanted? Use one or two stories from the room to make "problems prevent unnecessary investment" real.

Mini-lecture0:15–0:35

Not every problem is a business

“I don't like waking up early.”

A real problem for someone. But not automatically a viable business, so we investigate before we decide.

Investigate first

Who experiences the problem?
How often does it happen?
How serious is it?
What do people do about it now?
What's weak about that solution?

Later in the course: would people pay for a better solution?

Ask: is "I don't like waking up early" a business? Let people argue both sides, then land the point: people have thousands of problems a day, and not every one deserves a company.

The five questions are the Day 1 validation approach. Willingness to pay is a useful extension for later business-model work. It is not something participants must prove today.

SlowExpensiveUnreliableHard to accessAnnoying

This is the heart of Day 1, so slow down.

Who? Do not accept vague answers. "Young people" becomes "Young graduates between 20 and 30 looking for their first formal job".

How today? The existing workaround is the competition. For customer debts it might be notebooks, phone notes, WhatsApp messages, memory or spreadsheets.

Why weak? Is it too expensive, slow, inconvenient, unreliable, hard to access, hard to understand, time-consuming, or unavailable in some places? The curriculum asks whether the current solution is weak, slow, expensive or annoying.

Mini-lecture0:15–0:35

Example: the market food seller

Customers often arrive to find the food they wanted is finished. They'd like to know what's available before they travel.

Who has the problem?

Customers who regularly buy prepared food.

How do they solve it today?

They travel to the market or call the seller.

Why is that weak?

They can waste time travelling, only to find it's sold out.

A possible idea, still unvalidated: a simple WhatsApp ordering and availability service

Walk through the three answers slowly, one at a time. Ask the room what else customers might do today that isn't on the slide.

Point out the idea chip at the bottom: we have an idea, but we haven't validated it yet. First we understand the problem.

If the class has other local examples (market delivery, WhatsApp coordination for spare parts), invite them in.

Activity 21:20–1:50

Five traps to catch in yourself

You might sayAsk instead
“I want to build an app.”What problem would it solve?
“I want to solve unemployment.”Which people? Which part of it?
“People need this.”How do I know?
“Nobody solves this yet.”What do people do today?
“I just like this problem.”Interest is a start. Where's my evidence?

Go through quickly. For each one, ask the room whether they've caught themselves doing it today. Keep it friendly.

Your response to each mistake: (1) "That's potentially a solution. Let's identify the problem first." (2) "Which people? What specific part?" (3) "How do you know?" (4) "A problem may already have several competing solutions." (5) "Personal interest can be useful, but the problem still needs investigating."

Reminder of your role: you are not telling anyone which business to start. You are helping them discover and investigate problems themselves.

Before Day 2: talk to two people

Find at least two real people who experience your problem. Ask them:

1What exactly is the problem?
2How often does it happen?
3How do you handle it today?
4What do you dislike about that?
5What would you want instead?

Don't sell your idea. Your job is to listen.

Write down how they currently deal with the problem. Ask what they actually do, not whether they'd like your idea.

The assignment: speak with at least two real people who experience the chosen problem, and record how they currently deal with it.

Tell them: "Don't try to sell your idea during the interview. Your job is to listen." The purpose is to learn whether the problem exists from the perspective of the people who live it.

Practical tip: ask about the last time it happened and what they did, rather than hypothetical questions like "would you use an app for this?"
